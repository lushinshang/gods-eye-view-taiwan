# China Coast Guard (中国海警局, CCG) MMSI Patterns and Public Vessel Lists

## Do CCG vessels use a consistent MMSI prefix/pattern distinct from merchant/fishing vessels?

### Takeaway
There is **no official, documented CCG-specific MMSI numbering rule**. However, (a)-type (directly sourced) examples show a strong empirical cluster: a large number of tracked CCG vessels carry MMSIs in the **413875xxx** range (MID 413 + "875" sub-block), but this is not universal — other CCG hull numbers carry MMSIs under MID 412 with no shared sub-block, and at least one observed CCG AIS broadcast used a placeholder/spoofed MMSI (412000000) unrelated to any real registration. This is a pattern inferred from aggregator data, not a confirmed PRC-government-documented rule.

### Inferences
- The **413875xxx** sub-block (MID 413 + digits "875") appears repeatedly across many different CCG hull-number series (21xx, 25xx, 31xx, 35xx, 59xx, 61xx, 63xx, 65xx, 136xx, 325xx) spanning vessels built across roughly a decade, which suggests a real assignment convention used by whoever (likely the vessel's AIS equipment installer/registration authority) assigns these MMSIs to the CCG fleet — but this is an inference from aggregator data, not a confirmed/sourced administrative rule.
- The pattern is not airtight: at least one CCG hull number (1106) appears under a different MID/sub-block (412371217) in the data found, and the IMO number attached to it looks malformed, raising the possibility of aggregator data error rather than a genuine second CCG MMSI convention. This should be treated as unverified rather than disproof of the 413875xxx pattern.
- Several listed "IMO numbers" for CCG vessels (1235456, 3250400) do not match the standard 7-digit IMO check-digit format, indicating that at least some vessel-tracking aggregators populate placeholder or synthetic IMO values for naval/law-enforcement vessels not in Lloyd's/IHS Markit registries. This means IMO-MMSI cross-references from these sites should not be fully trusted without corroboration.

### Gaps
- No search surfaced an **official PRC** document, ITU filing, or Chinese Coast Guard Bureau regulation explicitly stating a dedicated MMSI sub-range for CCG vessels. The 413875xxx clustering is purely an empirical observation across third-party AIS aggregators (TradLinx, MagicPort, Maritime Optima, VesselFinder), which themselves sometimes disagree (e.g., CHINACOASTGUARD5901 shown with two different MMSIs across sources).
- No peer-reviewed academic paper or AMTI/C4ADS report was found that explicitly documents or confirms a "413875" or similar CCG-specific MMSI sub-block as a named finding (see further below — AMTI's own published methodology work is about militia fishing vessels, not CCG cutters specifically).
- Could not confirm whether the 413875xxx CCG vessels' MMSIs were self-assigned, assigned by a flag-state radio licensing process, or assigned by whichever AIS unit vendor equips Chinese government vessels.

## Has a maritime security research org / think tank / journalism outlet published analysis of CCG AIS/MMSI behavior?

### Takeaway
Yes — CSIS's **Asia Maritime Transparency Initiative (AMTI)**, in partnership with **C4ADS** and using commercial AIS data from **MarineTraffic** and **Starboard Maritime Intelligence**, has repeatedly published AIS-based tracking of CCG patrol activity (ship-days, locations), and AMTI has published a specific MMSI-to-vessel-name table for Chinese maritime militia fishing vessels (not CCG cutters) as an example of its methodology. No report found names a CCG-specific MMSI numbering convention as a headline finding.

### Cited Findings
- AMTI's 2023 report "Control by Patrol: The China Coast Guard in 2023" draws on commercial AIS providers **MarineTraffic and Starboard Maritime Analytics** to count CCG ship-days at South China Sea features — [AMTI](https://amti.csis.org/control-by-patrol-the-china-coast-guard-in-2023/)
- AMTI's 2024 report states that because AIS data is incomplete, its patrol-day figures "are minimum estimates, with the true level of coast guard presence likely higher," and separately notes "some CCG vessels' AIS transceivers may be disabled, remain undetected on commercial monitoring platforms, or transmitted incomplete or erroneous data" — [AMTI, "China Coast Guard Patrols in 2024"](https://amti.csis.org/china-coast-guard-patrols-in-2024-an-exercise-in-futility/)
- AMTI's 2025 report "Eyes on the Prize: CCG Patrols Prioritize Scarborough in 2025" continues AIS-based patrol tracking — [AMTI](https://amti.csis.org/eyes-on-the-prize-ccg-patrols-prioritize-scarborough-in-2025/)
- AMTI's Pratas Island analysis (2021–2025) found **24 unique CCG and China Maritime Safety Administration (MSA) vessels** observed broadcasting AIS, or reported via Taiwan Coast Guard Administration press releases, accounting for 94 total ship-days — AIS coverage is explicitly supplemented by official Taiwanese government reporting because AIS alone is incomplete — [AMTI, "Patrolling Pratas"](https://amti.csis.org/patrolling-pratas-from-peripheral-concern-to-a-new-normal/)
- AMTI, in partnership with C4ADS, used C4ADS AIS data/analytics to monitor ship movements at Luconia Shoals off Sarawak, Malaysia (vessel type not restricted to CCG) — referenced in search results tied to [AMTI "Tracking China's Coast Guard off Borneo"](https://amti.csis.org/tracking-chinas-coast-guard-off-borneo/)
- AMTI's "Pulling Back the Curtain on China's Maritime Militia" / Hainan militia update pulled **historical AIS records from commercial provider Starboard Maritime Intelligence for over 1,000 fishing vessels** with the Hainan-homeport "Qiong" name prefix, publishing a full table of **vessel names, MMSI numbers, and an "identification level"** for each — this is the clearest documented AMTI methodology for MMSI-to-vessel mapping, but it targets **militia fishing vessels, not CCG law-enforcement cutters** — [AMTI, "Behind the Curtain: An Update on Hainan's Maritime Militia"](https://amti.csis.org/behind-the-curtain-an-update-on-hainans-maritime-militia/)
- C4ADS's November 2021 "Pulling Back the Curtain" report (PDF) covers related dark-fleet/militia AIS analysis — [C4ADS PDF](https://c4ads.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/PullingBacktheCurtain-Report.pdf)
- A Chinese government-affiliated think tank, **SCSPI (South China Sea Strategic Situation Probing Initiative)**, has published a critique arguing AMTI's AIS-based patrol-counting approach risks overinterpretation because AIS data cannot capture intent, rules of engagement, or patrol outcomes — presented as a rebuttal/opposing view, not an independent verification — [SCSPI](https://www.scspi.org/en/node/732)
- CSIS AMTI separately investigated the fishing vessel Yuemaobinyu 42212 using open-source AIS; its AIS broadcasts reportedly stopped shortly after it returned to Bohe Port on June 14 (year not specified in source) — this is a militia/fishing vessel case study, illustrating AIS "going dark" behavior relevant to the broader reliability question, not a CCG example — [AMTI, "Seeking Clues in the Case of the Yuemaobinyu 42212"](https://amti.csis.org/seeking-clues-in-the-case-of-the-yuemaobinyu-42212/)

### Inferences
- AMTI's standard practice is to combine multiple commercial AIS feeds (MarineTraffic + Starboard) specifically because any single feed under-detects CCG vessels — implying commercial AIS coverage of CCG vessels is inconsistent across providers, consistent with the finding above that different aggregators show different MMSIs for the same vessel.
- AMTI's Hainan-militia MMSI table is the most concrete *published* precedent for building a name↔MMSI mapping table in this research area, but it was built for militia fishing vessels (name prefix "Qiong"), not CCG cutters; a similar CCG-specific table was not found in the search.

### Gaps
- No direct access to AMTI's full published Hainan-militia MMSI table (gated behind the report page; would require a follow-up fetch of the full AMTI feature to extract the table itself).
- No C4ADS report found that specifically targets CCG cutters' AIS/MMSI identity (C4ADS's named AIS-spoofing work found, per below, concerns a broader "Joint Analytical Cell" report on MMSI spoofing, not CCG-specific).
- Did not locate a RAND or academic (Google Scholar) paper specifically on CCG AIS/MMSI patterns within the search budget used; this is a gap that would need a dedicated follow-up search (e.g., "China Coast Guard" "automatic identification system" site:scholar.google.com).

## Do CCG vessels broadcast AIS at all, and what MMSI values have been observed for specific hull numbers?

### Takeaway
Yes — CCG vessels are documented broadcasting AIS regularly (enabling AMTI's multi-year patrol-tracking work and the many aggregator listings above), but AIS transmission is **not universal or fully reliable**: AMTI explicitly notes some CCG transceivers "may be disabled," some are "undetected on commercial monitoring platforms," and some incidents show CCG vessels broadcasting deliberately false identities rather than going fully dark.

### Cited Findings
- See hull-number-to-MMSI examples cited in the first section (2901, 3102, 3103, 3502, 3501, 5901, 6103, 6104, 6105, 6306, 6501, 32504, 13601, 2102, 2502, 3172/21601, 1104, 1106) — all sourced to specific aggregator pages above.
- AMTI 2024: "some CCG vessels' AIS transceivers may be disabled, remain undetected on commercial monitoring platforms, or transmitted incomplete or erroneous data," making patrol-day counts a floor, not a ceiling — [AMTI](https://amti.csis.org/china-coast-guard-patrols-in-2024-an-exercise-in-futility/)
- CCG cutter hull 5201 was confirmed broadcasting AIS (with a spoofed callsign) during an April 2023 Second Thomas Shoal incident — i.e., AIS was on, but identity was falsified, rather than AIS being switched off — [SeaLight](https://www.sealight.live/posts/gray-zone-tactics-playbook-spoofing)
- A December 2024 Philippine Coast Guard accusation names **CCG 21543** specifically, alleging its AIS track showed it in impossible simultaneous locations (Manila, Zambales, China, South Africa) within short timeframes over a 60-day AIS history — PCG spokesperson Jay Tarriela speculated nearby Chinese-flagged dredgers may have assisted the spoofing — [Maritime Executive, "Philippines Accuses Chinese Coast Guard of Using Dredgers for AIS Spoofing"](https://maritime-executive.com/article/philippines-accuses-chinese-coast-guard-of-using-dredgers-for-ais-spoofing); also reported at [LokmatTimes](https://www.lokmattimes.com/international/philippine-coast-guard-accuses-china-of-ais-spoofing-to-stir-public-alarm-in-zambales/amp)
- China disputed a related but separate February 2024 PCG claim that a CCG vessel blocked a Philippine vessel's AIS signal near Scarborough Shoal, with a Chinese academic (National Institute for South China Sea Studies) calling it fake, per Global Times — [GlobalSecurity.org reposting Global Times](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2024/02/mil-240226-globaltimes01.htm)

### Inferences
- The co-existence of (a) many CCG hull numbers with stable, long-running, trackable MMSIs across aggregators and (b) multiple documented false-identity broadcasts from specific hull numbers (5201, 21543) implies CCG AIS behavior is **vessel/mission-dependent**: routine patrol vessels broadcast real identities most of the time, while vessels engaged in confrontational operations (shadowing, blocking resupply missions) have been caught broadcasting falsified AIS identities.

### Gaps
- No systematic count found of what percentage of the ~160+ CCG fleet broadcasts AIS consistently versus goes dark; AMTI's own caveats acknowledge this is unknown and patrol counts are treated as a floor.

## Is there evidence of MMSI spoofing / AIS manipulation / multiple or changing MMSIs by the same CCG vessel?

### Takeaway
Yes, directly sourced evidence exists of CCG identity spoofing via AIS, including at least one case of falsified MMSI (not just name/callsign) and one case of alleged location spoofing, though both come from a single secondary analysis source (SeaLight) and a Philippine government accusation (not independently verified by a third party in the sources found) — this materially undermines confidence in any clean MMSI-based classification scheme for CCG vessels.

### Cited Findings
- CCG cutter near Second Thomas Shoal (April 18, 2023) broadcast a false identity "G" with MMSI **412000000**, a known placeholder-type MMSI shared by many unrelated vessels globally — a textbook identity-spoofing case with a concrete, documented MMSI — [SeaLight, "Gray Zone Tactics Playbook: Spoofing"](https://www.sealight.live/posts/gray-zone-tactics-playbook-spoofing)
- CCG cutter 5201 changed its broadcast callsign from "CCG5201" to "DONGYU1527-8 50%" on March 21, 2023 while shadowing BRP Malapascua — callsign/identity spoofing (not stated to involve an MMSI change specifically) — [SeaLight](https://www.sealight.live/posts/gray-zone-tactics-playbook-spoofing)
- SeaLight's own assessment: the author states they know of **no confirmed cases of Chinese security vessels spoofing their GPS location** in the South China Sea (as opposed to spoofing identity/callsign), even while noting location spoofing is well documented elsewhere in the world — an important distinction between **identity spoofing** (confirmed for CCG) and **location spoofing** (not confirmed for CCG, per this source) — [SeaLight](https://www.sealight.live/posts/gray-zone-tactics-playbook-spoofing)
- Philippine Coast Guard (Dec 2024) alleges CCG hull **21543**'s AIS track showed impossible simultaneous positions across Manila, Zambales, China, and South Africa — if accurate, this would be location spoofing, contradicting SeaLight's "no confirmed cases" statement; the two sources are **in direct tension**, and neither claim is independently cross-verified in the material found — [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/philippines-accuses-chinese-coast-guard-of-using-dredgers-for-ais-spoofing)
- A broader "Joint Analytical Cell" (referenced via C4ADS) released a report on "widespread MMSI spoofing activity" — found only as a headline/link, not fetched for CCG-specific content — [C4ADS](https://c4ads.org/?p=5628)
- Across the aggregator data pulled for CHINACOASTGUARD5901, two different sources list two different MMSIs (413875411 vs 413482360) for the **same IMO number** (9756028) — this is either an aggregator data error or evidence that the same hull has broadcast under more than one MMSI, but the sources don't resolve which — [Maritime Optima, 413875411](https://maritimeoptima.com/public/vessels/pages/imo:9756028/mmsi:413875411/CHINACOASTGUARD5901.html) vs [Maritime Optima, 413482360](https://maritimeoptima.com/public/vessels/pages/imo:9756028/mmsi:413482360/CHINACOASTGUARD5901.html)

### Inferences
- The direct contradiction between SeaLight's "no confirmed location-spoofing cases" statement and the PCG's December 2024 location-spoofing accusation against CCG 21543 means the location-spoofing question for CCG specifically should be treated as **contested/unresolved**, not settled in either direction.
- The two-MMSI anomaly for CHINACOASTGUARD5901 (same IMO, different MMSI across sources) is a concrete illustration of why **any MMSI-based classification/lookup table for CCG vessels needs cross-validation against multiple independent AIS data providers and, ideally, IMO number or hull-number corroboration**, not single-source MMSI lookups.

### Gaps
- Could not verify whether the CHINACOASTGUARD5901 dual-MMSI discrepancy reflects (a) a genuine MMSI reassignment/change over the vessel's service life, (b) simultaneous transmission under two MMSIs, or (c) a database/scraping error at one of the two aggregator sites — would require pulling raw historical AIS message logs (e.g., from MarineTraffic history or a site like aiscatcher.org) to resolve.
- No independent (non-PCG, non-Chinese-state-media) technical verification found for either the CCG 21543 location-spoofing claim or the Chinese government's rebuttal of the February 2024 Scarborough Shoal AIS-blocking claim.
